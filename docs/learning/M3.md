# M3: the event study (the kill test)

**Result (2026-10-04): no edge at the daily horizon; gate G1 not passed.**
Buying at the next open after an order win and holding up to five sessions
lost about 1% against the market after costs, and about 0.5% before them,
in the holdout and in the earlier years alike. The full story and the
options are in `docs/research/M3_decision.md`. This is the study doing its
job: it cost a day of computing to learn what a live account would have
taught expensively.

## What was built, and why this design

M3 asks the cheapest possible question before anything expensive is built:
*after a company files an order win (or one of a few other event types),
does its stock beat the market by more than trading costs?* If not, M4 to M8
have no reason to exist.

- **Event taxonomy.** Filings are typed by versioned rules
  (`configs/event_taxonomy.yaml`): exchange categories first, text patterns
  only for the generic buckets. The version includes a hash of the file, so
  every result names the exact rules that typed its events.
- **Pre-registration.** `docs/research/M3_prereg.md` fixes the event types,
  entry and exit rules, benchmark, filters, costs, the train/test split and
  the pass criteria, and pins the hash of the config. It was committed
  before any return was computed. The study command refuses to run on an
  edited config.
- **The engine.** Entry at the first open after the filing was *available*
  (not when it happened), exits a fixed number of sessions later, return
  measured against the Nifty 500, costs subtracted. Filters remove what
  could not have been traded: illiquid names, penny stocks, days locked at a
  circuit limit, corporate actions the adjuster cannot price.
- **Statistics that respect the data.** Events on the same day share that
  day's market, so they are not independent: standard errors and the
  bootstrap cluster by event date. Several event types and horizons are
  tested, so p-values are corrected (Benjamini-Hochberg) and the reported
  confidence intervals are widened to match (false-coverage-rate intervals).

## Key concepts

- **Look-ahead bias.** Using anything that was not knowable at decision
  time. The guard is structural: every row has `available_at`, and research
  reads only through `AsOf`.
- **Abnormal return.** The stock's return minus the market's over the same
  window: what the event added, not what the whole market did that day.
- **Multiple testing.** Test twenty ideas at 5% and one "works" by luck.
  Controlling the false discovery rate keeps the share of lucky findings
  among the reported ones low.
- **The holdout is spent once.** The test period is looked at one time, for
  one registered design. A second look is a second trial and is counted.

## What could go wrong

- Backfilled filings have no observed arrival time; the study treats the
  exchange's timestamp as availability and says so. Live-recorded data is
  the stricter test.
- Survivorship: a company delisted before recording began can be missing
  from the security master, so its filings stay unlinked and drop out of
  the study. The run counts unlinked events; if their share is large in the
  early years, the result leans towards companies that survived. This must
  be read off the real run's numbers, and it is not corrected for.
- A pass here is necessary, not sufficient: daily data hides the intraday
  path, which is what M5 measures.

## What the result taught

- **The sign matters as much as the size.** A negative drift after good
  news says the open already overshot: the buyers at the open are late.
- **Check the yardstick.** Every filing type was negative, so a placebo on
  ordinary days was run: a small tilt (about 0.1%), not the explanation.
- **A failed gate is a decision point, not a bug.** Changing the rules
  until something passes is the one move that makes every later number
  meaningless.
